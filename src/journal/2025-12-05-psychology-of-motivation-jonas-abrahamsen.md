We're heading into winter base training season, the time of year when **consistency beats intensity** and small habits shape future performances.

If you're feeling the winter dip in motivation, you're not alone. This week's science section is all about mental performance and the psychology of staying engaged.

## Science Corner: Motivation and Focus in Endurance Athletes

Staying motivated isn't just about mental toughness. It is a **measurable set of cognitive skills** that elite athletes train. Current research in sports psychology highlights three areas that matter most for cyclists.

### 1. Self-regulation and attention control

Cycling requires managing discomfort and redirecting attention away from threats (pain, fatigue) and toward what you're doing on the bike (cadence, breathing, power).

**Key finding:** athletes who consciously shift attention between internal cues (breathing) and external cues (road position, wind direction) show higher pain tolerance and better performance. Research shows attention strategies reduce activity in the threat-processing regions of the brain.

*Reference: Brick NE, MacIntyre TE, Campbell MJ. Attentional focus in endurance activity: new perspectives on processes and performance. Psychology of Sport and Exercise, 2016.*

### 2. Motivation: intrinsic vs. extrinsic drive

Athletes who build their training around **intrinsic values** (mastery, identity, personal satisfaction) rather than external outcomes (race results, comparison) stay more satisfied over the long term and burn out less.

Self-Determination Theory research shows that intrinsic motivation leads to:

- Higher training consistency
- Improved well-being
- Greater resilience to setbacks

*Reference: Ryan RM, Deci EL. Self-Determination Theory: Basic Psychological Needs in Motivation, Development, and Wellness. Guilford Press, 2017.*

### 3. Cognitive reframing and reading your data

Cyclists often interpret sensations or numbers negatively without realising it: *"My legs feel heavy, today's ruined."* Studies show that reframing (*"This is fatigue; I can still execute the ride"*) improves performance by changing how we appraise the threat.

Reframing discomfort changes autonomic responses and improves endurance performance. Athletes who reframe bad rides see training setbacks as **adaptive stress, not failure**.

*Reference: Marcora SM. Perception of effort during exercise is independent of afferent feedback from skeletal muscles, heart, and lungs. Journal of Applied Physiology, 2009.*

## Practical Velo-Savvy Takeaways

Three strategies you can use immediately:

**Micro-goal setting.** Break sessions into small execution goals, for example *"Hold 90 rpm for the next 3 minutes,"* then repeat. This reduces perceived exertion and boosts motivation.

**Cognitive load management.** Heavy mental day? Reduce training intensity. Your brain draws from the same fatigue pool as your body.

**Purpose reconnection.** Once a week, write down: *"Why do I cycle?"* This strengthens intrinsic motivation and builds long-term focus.

## Bonus Coach Advice: Never Talk Yourself Down

Never say anything negative about yourself before or during a ride.

Have you ever been at the start of a group ride or race and heard people say *"I haven't been riding much," "I don't feel good today"* or *"My legs are tired"*? **This is a habit that separates amateurs from pros.** At the start of a professional race, no one says negative things about themselves. Telling people you're not feeling good plants the seeds of giving up.

Last week I broke my own rule on a ride and told a friend I wasn't feeling good. Sure enough, I only felt worse after that and was soon dropped. **Don't say negatives about yourself!**

## Athlete Spotlight: Jonas Abrahamsen

![Jonas Abrahamsen descending the Tourmalet in the polka-dot jersey, Tour de France 2024](/assets/images/journal/riders/jonas-abrahamsen.jpg)
*Photo: Hugo LUC, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jonas_Abrahamsen_in_the_descent_of_Tourmalet_pass_during_stage_14_of_TDF_24.jpg), via Wikimedia Commons*

**Jonas Abrahamsen** is a Norwegian professional road racer riding for **Uno-X Mobility** (UCI ProTeam). He stands 1.83 m and races at around 78 kg: a rouleur and breakaway specialist build. His style is aggressive and opportunistic, often attacking from breakaways, and he's known for tenacity and tactical sense.

### Career breakthroughs

- **2020:** first elite-level win, a stage (plus the mountains classification) at the Tour of Małopolska.
- **2024:** his breakout season. He won the **Brussels Cycling Classic** with a decisive solo attack. In the opening days of the Tour de France he led both the **mountains (polka-dot)** classification, becoming the first Norwegian ever to wear that jersey, and the **points (green)** classification for several stages.
- **2025:** just weeks after breaking his collarbone, he went on a long-range breakaway on **Stage 11 of the Tour de France**, rode 158 km in a small group, and took his first Tour stage win.

That comeback resonated. After the win, Jonas said he cried in hospital after the crash, but got back on the rollers the next day and committed to making the Tour start. Then he delivered.

### What makes him interesting

**Breakaway-specialist mindset.** Jonas doesn't attack just to survive; he attacks to win from unexpected moves. That style demands high resilience, willingness to take risks, excellent pacing and the ability to suffer, all ideal traits for endurance athletes.

**Resilience and comeback.** A collarbone break and a short recovery into the Tour de France is a story of grit and disciplined rehab, and a prime example of how **setbacks + mindset + preparation can equal triumph**.

**All-round power and endurance.** At 78 kg with a muscular build, he challenges the idea that only light climbers succeed. For riders who aren't naturally light, he's a model: strength, power and all-round ability still lead to big results.

**Tactical intelligence and opportunism.** Winning a classic solo, leading jerseys at the Tour, taking a Tour stage: these demand race IQ, attention and split-second decisions as much as strength. Great lessons for any Velo-Savvy rider working on race craft.
